Can a computer really hear an acoustic instrument?

Acoustic Ear investigates microphone-first automatic music transcription: understanding real musical performance directly from ordinary recorded audio, without requiring a pickup, MIDI instrument, or proprietary input device.

01 / Research question

Music is messy.
That is the point.

Automatic music transcription asks a deceptively simple question: what notes are being played, and when?

Real acoustic performance complicates the answer. Strings keep ringing. Harmonics overlap. Rooms add reflections. Microphones color the signal. Fast passages, articulation, chords, and background noise create ambiguity that clean electrical or symbolic inputs avoid.

This project begins with acoustic guitar and asks whether useful musical understanding can come directly from an ordinary microphone. The long-term question is broader: can the same approach generalize across acoustic instruments and voices?

02 / Methodology

A baseline before a breakthrough.

Existing systems are measured first. Every added model, dataset, or complication has to justify itself with evidence.

01

Freeze the baseline

Evaluate existing pretrained transcription systems before fine-tuning or architectural changes.

02

Record the real world

Capture controlled, unamplified performances through consumer microphone hardware.

03

Build traceable data

Use original recordings, documented public datasets, and procedurally generated labeled audio with explicit provenance.

04

Adapt deliberately

Target observed failure modes with transfer learning, augmentation, or architecture changes rather than guessing.

05

Test what was unseen

Judge changes on held-out real recordings, not on the material used to adapt the model.

06

Raise the difficulty

Progress from isolated notes toward resonance, chords, techniques, rapid passages, and additional instruments.

What counts as evidence

Measured, not imagined.

No result is published simply because an output looks plausible.

04 / Research principles

Make it reproducible.
Keep it honest.

01

Preserve baselines

Never erase the reference point that tells us whether a change actually helped.

02

Track data lineage

Record where training material came from, how it was transformed, and which restrictions follow it.

03

Separate research from product claims

Research-only datasets and checkpoints remain clearly isolated from commercially compatible work.

04

Prefer held-out reality

Synthetic data can teach and stress-test a model, but it cannot substitute for unseen real recordings.

05

Report failures

Bad runtime, false notes, broken environments, and failed experiments are evidence too.

05 / Long-term direction

From hearing notes
to understanding musicians.

If microphone-first transcription can become accurate and fast enough, the same foundation could support interactive music learning, automatic transcription, practice feedback, performance analysis, and discovery tools across guitar, bass, piano, drums, voice, banjo, mandolin, and more.

About the project

Independent, early, and intentionally transparent.

Acoustic Ear is a self-directed undergraduate research project by a student at Calhoun Community College. The project is independently conducted and is not presented as an official college research program.

AI coding and research tools assist with implementation, literature review, debugging, and experimental infrastructure. Research questions, experimental direction, recordings, interpretation, and decisions remain human-directed.

The development repository is private while the experimental foundation is still changing rapidly. This public page documents the research question, method, progress, and eventually the measured results.
